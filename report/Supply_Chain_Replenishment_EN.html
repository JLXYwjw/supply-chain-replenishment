Part 1 · Business Objective

1. Why this analysis

Retail/manufacturing supply chains balance lost orders from stockouts against cash tied up in overstock. Core questions:

  • Inventory health: which SKU/warehouse is near the reorder point (stockout risk) or far above demand (cash tied up)?
  • Replenishment strategy: SKUs with volatile demand and long lead time need higher safety stock / reorder point.

One-line goal: use demand volatility + lead time + inventory coverage to identify the SKUs/warehouses to watch and give a replenishment direction.

Honest boundary (stated up front). This data is a synthetic grid (50×5×365, no missing): ① the stockout label is all 0, no positives → stockout classification impossible; ② the demand-forecast column correlates with sales 0.949 → demand is already "given the answer", retraining a predictor would be self-deceiving. So this project does not fake predictions; it provides decision support for inventory health & replenishment strategy.

Part 4 · Replenishment Modeling

4. Demand-volatility clustering + safety-stock formula (principle · why · how)

4.1 Why clustering instead of stockout classification

This data has stockout labels all 0 (no positives), so a stockout classifier cannot be trained. Instead, unsupervised clustering: group SKU×warehouses by demand mean, volatility CV, lead time, inventory coverage to identify similar replenishment-profile groups, and design a differentiated strategy per group — implementable without labels.

4.2 Safety stock / reorder point formula (statistical principle)

Two classic replenishment statistics (principle explained):

  • Safety stock SS = z · σ_L: σ_L is the std of demand within lead time (= daily demand σ × √lead time), z is the service-level Z-quantile (e.g. 90% service z≈1.28, 95% z≈1.65). The more volatile, the longer the lead, the higher the required service → the larger the safety stock.
  • Reorder point ROP = d·L + SS: d daily demand × L lead time = expected consumption during lead, plus a safety buffer.

How. This report computes SS and ROP per SKU×warehouse with volatility-tiered z (high CV>0.4 → z=1.65, else 1.28), then ranks by inventory − ROP gap to get the replenishment priority (P1–P4 in Part 3).

Why not deep learning. Tabular, 50×5=250 groups, no stockout labels; clustering + statistical formula is explainable and directly implementable (the inventory team can explain "why this SKU needs replenishment"). DL has no labels here and is less explainable, with limited gain — consistent with "use only when the business needs it".
